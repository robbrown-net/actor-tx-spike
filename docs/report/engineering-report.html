1. Where we started

The idea. Rob wanted to learn Elixir, the BEAM and OTP by exploring stateful transaction messages passed around stateful sub-systems, in the spirit of Fred Brooks: pick the most productive high-level language that fits, but keep an escape route (Rust) if performance demands it.

The first spike (built in Cursor):

  • One gen_statem per transaction: queued → awaiting_a → awaiting_b → committed, with compensating → failed.
  • Two fake subsystem GenServers under a supervisor, idempotent per tx_id, with a "crash next request" switch.
  • A disk-backed snapshot store so a node restart could resume in-flight work.
  • Three tests. 76.5% line coverage.

It worked, and it proved the OTP shape: a process per transaction, supervised workers, restart on crash. But the analysis found 12 gaps worth recording because they set the agenda: idempotency lived only in memory; resubmitting after restart re-executed; finished processes never exited; await timeouts crashed the caller; compensation was a stub; the store rewrote everything per write, non-atomically; one corrupt snapshot could stop boot; A and B were hardcoded; the README claimed a vendored toolchain that the repository did not contain.

2. The questions that shaped everything

"Orchestrator or choreography?"

The owner asked us to settle this first, by clarifying the business need. The answers decided it:

AnswerImplication
Money, inventory, legal recordsHalf-done is unacceptable; audit trail and compensation are mandatory
Flow varies per transactionRouting must be data, not code
Parallel fan-out and branchingReal workflow semantics
"Where is transaction X now?"One place must know the whole story
Change flow without redeployingThe flow lives outside the participants
Scale across nodesParticipants addressed logically

→ Orchestration, with flows as declarative data. (ADR 0001.)

"What do the sub-systems actually are?"

The pivotal reframing came next. The sub-systems are facades over unreliable third-party providers: an FX swap and an idempotent payment. They may be fast and cheap, slow, expensive, degraded, stalled, or change behaviour without telling us. The system must weigh cost and speed, keep going when a provider fails, and eventually be self-healing and real-time worldwide, watching CPU, memory, storage and network.

That turned "run A then B" into provider selection under uncertainty. The architecture, the fakes, the health model and the scoring all follow from this.

4. How the design works now

submit ─► Payment (one process per transaction)
            │ quote  ─► Selector ─► Provider.Server ×N ─► adapter
            │ policy: weights, constraints, max bps from best
            ├─ accept ► authorise ► pay        (idempotency key per step)
            ├─ definite failure ─► undo, fail over to next candidate
            ├─ doubt ─► ask provider (status) ─► never guess
            └─ unresolvable payment ─► needs_review
  • Selection (Selector, Policy): concurrent quotes within a deadline; stalled and

quarantined providers are not called; constraints, then a relative cost/speed/reliability score; deterministic ranking; baseline fallback.

  • Provider layer (Provider.Server, Provider.Health): breaker, rolling stats,

bulkhead, enforced timeouts, version-drift quarantine, gradual recovery.

  • Lifecycle (Payment): retries, compensation, failover, reconciliation, escalation.
  • Narrative (Events, Narrative, Panel): every component announces itself; the

dashboard and terminal are projections.

6. Architecture steers (what the owner pushed on)

  • Declarative data over code for flows and policy.
  • "Keep going": the system always starts with a valid result and may only improve it; unavailable providers must be routed around.
  • Single node first, but do not design distribution out.
  • Compensation as real operations and a hard price-distance constraint in policy.
  • Cascading objectives: global → merchant (paid privilege) → special customer, plus weekly standing guarantees.
  • Two-leg lifecycle: quote, accept, authorise, pay, then settlement phases with regional cut-offs (e.g. 18:00 UK local).
  • A sentinel that checks provider version, up-state and response time on a configurable cadence.
  • Gradual recovery rather than snap-back.

User guide

For the person running and watching the system: an operator, a tester, a compliance reviewer or someone learning how it behaves. No programming needed. (Developers: DEVELOPER_GUIDE. Testers who want the full picture of the checks: TESTING.)

1. What it does

You hand it a payment: an amount to convert and pay. It asks several third-party providers for an FX quote, picks the best under your rules (price, speed, reliability, jurisdiction), accepts it, authorises, pays and settles, and keeps a tamper-evident record of everything. If a provider misbehaves it routes around it. If it cannot be sure a payment did or did not happen, it never guesses: it stops and asks a person.

Everything here runs against five fake providers with different prices and speeds, so you can break things safely.

3. The dashboard

Five tabs: Live, Performance, Bulk send, Review and Test lab.

Live. The five providers (price, speed, health state, circuit breaker), the transactions in flight and finished, the Story pane (what each component is doing, in plain words), and the Economics panel (what the system cost, what better rates saved, margin against guarantees). Click a transaction to follow just its story; show all clears the filter.

Header controls. Amount, merchant, customer, region, instrument (spot or forward), customer type (business or retail) and the From and To currencies, then Submit transaction. The currencies now matter: a pair is priced from the market (USD to INR quotes near 83), only providers that carry both currencies are asked (each provider card says what it carries), and some regions restrict currencies (INR cannot be used in China). See architecture/12. Auto traffic submits transactions for you. Region matters: IN sends records only to the India log and allows only India-eligible providers; CN likewise for China; forwards to IN and retail to CN are refused before any provider is asked.

Breaking providers. On each provider card, buttons change its behaviour:

ButtonWhat the provider does

Policy and cut-offs. Apply policy changes the routing weights (cost, speed, reliability) and limits. Set cut-off for region sets a settlement deadline some seconds ahead, to see a transaction stop and undo when it passes; Clear removes it.

Reading a transaction's story. Typical order: received, eligibility, policy, deadline, quotes asked and answered, selected X, accept, authorise, pay, settle, completed. A transaction ends completed, failed (cleanly: nothing left half done) or needs_review (a person must decide; money may have moved).

Performance. CPU, memory, network, scheduler load, run queue, throughput and end-to-end latency, per second and rolled up per minute.

4. Bulk send

The Bulk send tab sends many transactions for you. It works with a plain mix demo.

  • Batch: how many (buttons for 10, 100, 1,000 and 10,000, or any number from 1 to 10,000), the

smallest and largest amount (each transaction gets a random amount in that range), the pause between transactions in milliseconds (0 sends as fast as it can), and an optional seed.

  • Send with the header settings: every transaction uses the merchant, customer, region, type

and currencies chosen in the header; only the amount varies.

  • Randomise everything: every transaction draws its own merchant, customer, origin and

destination region, instrument, customer type and currencies at random. Untick a setting to keep it as in the header. Some random combinations are refused on purpose (a forward to IN, a retail customer to CN): they show as failed because the system refused them before any provider was asked. A seed makes a random batch repeat exactly.

  • Shuffle the header settings: sets the header controls to random values once, so you can submit a single random transaction by hand.
  • Progress: state (running, draining while the last ones finish, finished, stopped), a bar, and

sent, in flight, completed, failed (including refused), needs review, submit errors, elapsed time and rate. Stop ends the sending (transactions already sent finish normally); Clear forgets a finished batch. Click a latest transaction id to see its story on the Live tab.

  • Sender waits at N in flight: the sender pauses while that many of its own transactions are still running (0 = no limit).
  • Back-pressure: admission limit: the most transactions the system will run at once. Beyond it a submission is refused

as overloaded before anything starts, and the sender waits and tries again (the throttled count). Type a number and press Apply, or No limit. The panel shows how many are running, admitted, refused and the peak.

  • Why they did not complete: the progress panel groups the failure and review messages and counts them.

One batch runs at a time. With no limit, a large burst with no pause overwhelms the demo providers: in a 2,000-transaction test only 30% completed (the rest failed cleanly). With a limit of 100 to 150, 99% or more completed. Try both and watch the numbers. Details: architecture/09, architecture/10 and the measurements in quality/LOAD_RESULTS.

Measuring from the command line

mix load --count 2000 --admission none,200,150,100,50     # one run per limit, then a table
mix load --count 2000 --admission 200 --sender-cap 120    # the sender holds itself to 120 in flight

Add --pace 0.2 (provider speed), --mode random --seed 7 (randomised settings), --interval MS. It prints sent, completed, failed, needs review, throttled, refused, peak, seconds and the reasons for each run.

5. Review

The Review tab (the number in brackets is how many are waiting) holds every transaction the system would not settle by guessing: a payment it could not confirm, a settlement or undo it could not complete, or the audit log failing after money moved. Break a provider (pay vanishes, settlement fails) and send a transaction to see one.

Pick a transaction on the left. On the right you see why it stopped, the provider, the steps done, its story and what people have already done. Then:

  1. Type your name (it is remembered). Everything you do is recorded under it.
  2. Check with the provider asks what it holds for each step (read-only).
  3. Send again: settle, or an undo (cancel the accept, void the authorisation, refund). It is sent under its original key, so repeating it cannot repeat the effect. Repair the provider first (heal) if it was refusing.
  4. Resolve with an outcome and a reason: paid (the money moved and it is whole), not paid (nothing moved), or written off (it cannot be told, the exposure is accepted). A decision without a reason is refused.

Each action is written to the audit log (in the transaction's own regional log) before it takes effect; if the log is unavailable nothing is sent. After a restart the queue is rebuilt from the logs. Read it from a log: mix replay reviews audit/IN.log. Details: architecture/11.

6. The Test lab

mix demo --lab, tab Test lab. It lets you provoke the hard situations and see a verdict.

  • Scenarios: 17 ready-made situations, each with what to expect and checks with evidence. Run

one or Run all. Click a transaction id to see its story on the Live tab. Groups: jurisdiction routing, idempotency, provider faults, audit-log downtime, restarts and the router.

  • Audit log: Healthy, Down, or Fails after N steps. See what each transaction state does

when the record cannot be kept: a transaction that has not started fails cleanly; one that has moved money stops for a person. Kill the router and watch the supervisor bring it back. Verify logs + residency checks every log's chain, every transaction's rules and where each transaction is recorded.

  • Crash and recover: Arm a crash at an exact moment (for example "when authorise succeeds"),

submit a transaction, then Plan recovery (dry run) to see what the log says about each open transaction, then Recover now.

  • Idempotency: Submit it twice (same id) and Send again, same key (re-send a completed step):

the provider must do nothing more.

  • What the providers actually did: the ground truth the checks are judged against.

Without --lab the tab explains how to turn it on. More detail: architecture/08.

7. The audit log: verify, replay, recover

Run with a kept log (mix demo --audit-dir audit/), then:

mix audit.verify audit/GLOBAL.log                # is the hash chain intact? prints the head to anchor
mix replay verify audit/GLOBAL.log               # did the day follow all 11 rules? (non-zero exit if not)
mix replay txs audit/IN.log                      # one line per transaction
mix replay at audit/GLOBAL.log --n 300           # the system as it stood at audit record 300 (or --ms, --seq)
mix replay whatif audit/GLOBAL.log --set max_bps_from_best=25    # a different policy on a real day
mix replay diff audit/GLOBAL.log other.log       # compare two systems' days
mix replay residency audit/                      # is every transaction recorded only where it may be?
mix replay reviews audit/IN.log                  # transactions that went to review: open, or who decided what and why
mix replay batches audit/GLOBAL.log              # the bulk batches sent: plan and result
  • Verify shows whether anyone altered the record. Replay re-judges the day with the same

decision code that made it. What-if says how many transactions would route differently and the net effect (quote-level: it assumes the alternative would have completed).

  • Restart: start again with the same --audit or --audit-dir. Transactions that were open are

resumed from where the log says they stood, never paid twice; anything the log cannot settle goes to needs_review. Messages beginning recovery: appear in the terminal.

  • A log that fails verification is refused: the system will not append to a damaged record.

9. Jurisdictions

The eleven jurisdictions in scope are UK, EU, US, India, Mexico, Brazil, Turkey, Saudi Arabia, Japan, South Korea and China, for spot and forward, business and retail. The demo implements rules for IN and CN (local-only logs and provider restrictions) and retention periods for UK and EU. The country-by-country picture, what evidence exists and what is missing, is in compliance/COMPLIANCE_EVIDENCE. The rules are unverified by counsel: do not treat them as legal advice.

11. Words used

Quote: a provider's offered rate and fee. Net: what the customer ends up with (amount × rate − fee). Failover: moving to the next provider after a definite failure. Doubt: a call whose outcome is unknown. Compensation: undoing what was done at a provider. Guarantee: a merchant's promised rate for a period. Cut-off: the regional deadline for settlement. Residency: records stay in the regions they must. Batch: a run of many transactions sent from the Bulk send tab. Seed: a number that makes a random batch repeat exactly. Full glossary: architecture/02.

System refuses each further submission at once with overloaded and a suggested wait; nothing is started or recorded. 4. Caller waits and submits again. 5. As transactions end, slots free and refused callers get in.
Extensions2a. No limit is configured: everything is admitted and providers' bulkheads shed the excess (many fail cleanly: see the load results). 3a. A provider is busy at the quote stage: it is asked again every few milliseconds until the deadline. 3b. A provider is busy at accept, authorise, pay or settle: ask again after the backoff, no attempt used, until the deadline. 3c. Undo, status and probe calls use reserved capacity and are not refused while quotes are. 5a. A transaction's process dies: its slot is freed. 6. Operator changes the limit at run time (admission panel, mix demo --max-in-flight, or the control API).
Special requirementsA refusal is safe because nothing was done; a provider's overloaded answer is safe to retry because the call was never made.
StatusImplemented. See 10 and LOAD_RESULTS. Not built: a queue in front of the limit, per-merchant fairness, automatic tuning.

Operation contracts (key system operations)

submit(request, providers, policy, opts)

  • Pre: request.amount present; request.id contains no :; providers and policy supplied.
  • Post: a transaction process exists for id (or already existed); a tx_received event was emitted; returns {:ok, id}.

select(request, providers, policy, deadline)

  • Pre: policy valid.
  • Post: every provider is either skipped (state excluded), called, or reported failed; candidates satisfy all hard constraints and max_bps_from_best; ranking is deterministic (score desc, provider id asc); returns within deadline + grace.

step(op, key) (accept, authorise, pay)

  • Pre: previous step succeeded at the same provider; quote not expired.
  • Post: the effect has happened at most once for key; outcome is ok, definite failure, or doubt; doubt is never treated as failure for pay.

compensate(done_steps)

  • Post: each completed step has its undo issued in reverse order, or the transaction is in needs_review.

record(event) (audit)

  • Pre: the audit log verifies (or is empty).
  • Post: exactly one new line exists whose n is the previous n + 1, whose prev is the previous hash and whose hash covers its body; free-text fields contain no Luhn-valid card number; on a synced log the line is durable before the call returns.

verify(path)

  • Post: returns the record count and head hash if every sequence number, link and hash holds; otherwise the first failing line and the reason (malformed, out_of_sequence, chain_broken, hash_mismatch). Never modifies the log.

price(usage) (economics)

  • Post: each resource cost is an integer number of pico-units computed from the rate card with half-even rounding; the total is the sum of the parts; value (saved, margin) is kept separately in minor units and never mixed with cost until the net is formed.

evaluate(request, observations, policy) (selection, pure)

  • Pre: each observation names a provider, its health state, what it answered, the latency and when it was seen.
  • Post: returns the per-provider judgements, the admissible candidates ranked best first (score descending, provider id ascending) and the excluded with reasons. No process, clock or event is touched, so the live system and replay get the same answer from the same inputs.

state_at(day, point) (replay)

  • Post: only records up to and including point are folded; returns each transaction's summary as of then, who is in flight, provider health and last price, the policy in force and the operator actions so far.

what_if(day, overrides) (replay)

  • Pre: overrides form a valid policy change.
  • Post: every transaction with recorded quotes is re-judged under the recorded policy and under the changed one; the totals say how many would route differently and what that is worth in net and latency, on quote values.

barrier() (write-ahead)

  • Post: returns :ok only if every registered durable sink has written every event emitted before the call; otherwise an error naming the failed or unresponsive sink. Payment does not make a money-moving call unless it returned :ok.

decide(eligibility, request)

  • Post: permitted? is true only if every region involved allows the instrument and the customer type; the allowed-provider test is the conjunction of every region's provider rule; logs is the union of the involved regions' logs, or only the local-only regions' logs if any is involved; retention_days is the longest that applies.

plan(records) (recovery, pure)

  • Post: nil if the transaction never started; done if it has a terminal record; otherwise a mode saying what to do next. A step with a recorded start and no recorded answer is doubt, never next.

recover(days, opts)

  • Post: for each open transaction found (once, however many logs hold it) a resumed Payment is started, or an error is returned for it; the money rules of Payment apply unchanged.

6.3 Invariants the model must keep

  1. At most one pay effect per transaction.
  2. An effect row is unique per idempotency_key.
  3. A transaction's chosen_provider is the provider of its pay effect when outcome = completed.
  4. A failed transaction has no pay effect and every accept / authorise has a matching undo.
  5. needs_review is the only state that may hold un-undone effects, and it must carry the reason.
  6. Narrative seq is strictly increasing; events are never edited.

Principles:

  1. Sampling, not tracing, for resources. One sampler process, fixed cost regardless of load.
  2. In-process aggregation. Counters are cheap atomics / ETS counters; never a message per measurement.
  3. Bounded memory. Ring buffers with fixed capacity; observability must not become the leak.
  4. Tiered retention. Raw → 1 min → 1 h so long-term storage grows slowly and predictably.
  5. Alerts go into the narrative so the same dashboard explains what changed.
  6. Overhead budget: the monitoring layer must stay under ~1% CPU and a fixed memory cap, and is itself measured.

7.8 Cost of durability (added 2026-10-17)

Writing the log ahead of every money-moving call puts a disk flush on the transaction's critical path. Records are written immediately but made durable by group commit: one fsync on each barrier and at most 50 ms apart, so one fsync covers every record written meanwhile. Under load many transactions share a flush; without it the system would be capped by the disk's fsync rate (this was seen: a per-record fsync starved the barrier under a test-suite load). The barrier adds roughly one flush per money-moving step per transaction (four, plus undos). Measure it with the load mode on the roadmap before setting latency targets.

How the faults work

  • Audit log down / fails after N steps. Each transaction's durable-log barrier goes through the

lab (Lab.barrier_fun/1), which counts calls per transaction. Down refuses every step; after N lets the first N barriers through and then fails with disk_full. This is exactly the signal Payment already reacts to, so a transaction that has done nothing fails (audit_unavailable) and one that has moved money stops in needs_review.

  • Crash at an exact point. A watcher subscribes to the event stream and kills the transaction

process the moment the armed event happens (for example step_succeeded authorise, or step_started pay with the call already in flight), as a node dying at that instant would. The provider keeps its state, as a real provider would.

  • Recover. The plan is Recovery.plan over the logs; recovering is Recovery.recover.
  • Kill the router. The router is under a supervisor in lab mode. While it is down the barrier

fails (money stops); when it is restarted it re-registers and rebuilds its routing table.

  • Known conditions. Before each scenario the lab pauses price drift, fixes prices so cobalt

is the best provider, heals every provider, restores a healthy log and clears armed crashes, so scenarios are deterministic. It leaves pace at 0.3.

Behaviour

  • One batch at a time. Starting while one runs is refused. Stop ends the sending; transactions

already sent finish normally and are still counted. Clear forgets a finished batch.

  • Counting. A transaction is counted when its payment announces how it ended, so in flight

falls to zero only when everything has ended. While everything has been sent but some have not ended, the state is draining.

  • Randomised settings include impossible ones on purpose. Forwards to IN and retail customers

to CN are not permitted; those transactions are refused before any provider is asked and are counted as failed. A source currency is never the same as the destination currency.

  • Back-off. If the system answers a submission with overloaded (the admission limit is full), the same transaction is

held and sent again after the pause the system suggests; it counts as throttled, not as sent. With a sender cap the sender also pauses while that many of its own transactions are running (sender waits).

  • Why they did not complete. Failure and review messages are grouped (numbers taken out) and counted, top eight.
  • Seeds. With a seed, a random batch repeats exactly (same amounts, same settings, in the same

recorded in the audit log. They do not yet change pricing, routing or eligibility: the fake providers quote one rate per provider.

problem is reported together.

  • Demo.Bulk.Plan is pure: new/1 validates parameters into a plan, start/1 makes the random

Why a refused submission is safe

{:error, {:overloaded, ms}} is returned before the transaction has started: no provider has been asked, no audit record written, nothing to undo. 5. Conventions that matter

  • Money is integers. Amounts and fees are integer minor units; rates are integers scaled by

1e8; rounding is half-even; costs are pico-units. Parse decimals at the edge with Money. Never use floats for money. Display with Money.format/1.

  • Every provider call is idempotent and write-ahead. Calls carry stable keys

(<tx>:<provider>:<step>); Payment waits on Events.barrier/0 before a money-moving call. If the audit log cannot make the record durable, the call is not made.

  • Never fail over after doubt. An unresolved pay or settle goes to needs_review; it is never

retried at another provider.

  • Decisions are pure where possible (Policy, Selector.evaluate/3, Eligibility,

Recovery.plan/1, Provider.Health), so replay can reuse the same code as the live path.

  • Narrate everything through Events; record replay inputs with Journal (hidden from the

dashboard, kept in the audit log). Do not log card numbers: Audit scrubs free text, but do not rely on it.

  • **No String.to_atom, binary_to_term, Code.eval* on external data.**
  • Comments say why, not what. Keep the style of the surrounding code.

  1. A limit trades speed for completion. Raising it lowers the completion rate; lowering it costs time. The knee

  1. Completed per second is highest with no limit, and that is misleading: it counts the quick, cheap failures.

  1. The capacity is the quote fan-out. Every transaction asks all five providers, so capacity is about

(quote slots per provider ÷ provider latency) transactions per second, 60% of each bulkhead being available to quotes.

  1. A cap at the sender is gentler than refusals at the door (no throttled retries, same completion),

but only protects against that sender; the admission limit protects the system from everyone.

Decisions (ADRs)

ADR 0001: Orchestrated transactions with declarative flows

Status: accepted. Later decisions: ADRs 0002–0007 (safety rules, provider model, observability, tests, toolchain, cascade/guarantees/deadlines). Date: 2026-10-15

Context

The domain is money / inventory / legal records, so a transaction left half-done is unacceptable. Observed needs:

  • The flow varies per transaction (routing depends on payload and results).
  • Journeys need parallel fan-out/join and branching on results.
  • Operators must be able to ask "where is transaction X right now?".
  • The flow must be changeable without redeploying every subsystem.
  • Subsystems should eventually scale independently / across nodes.

Decision

  1. Orchestration, not choreography. One transaction actor per

transaction owns the journey. Subsystems are participants that react to commands; they do not know the flow.

  1. Flows are declarative data (validated into structs at load time):

steps, dependencies (fan-out/join), branch rules on results, and a compensation per step. The transaction process interprets the data; it contains no hardcoded subsystem names.

  1. Availability over consistency ("keep going"). Every transaction starts

with a valid baseline outcome and may only ever be replaced by a better valid outcome. A failure or partition leaves the best outcome found so far.

  1. Scope: single node first. Distribution is deferred but must not be

designed out (logical subsystem addressing, no node-local assumptions in the message contracts).

  1. Quality gates: 100% automated unit-test coverage; >85% automated

integration-test coverage.

Interpretation of decision 2 (please confirm)

  • Each flow defines a baseline outcome produced at queued without

contacting any subsystem.

  • Each step may yield a candidate outcome; the flow supplies an ordering

(better_than). A candidate replaces the current outcome only if better.

  • Because every intermediate outcome is valid by construction, a transaction

that exhausts retries or loses a participant terminates with its best outcome rather than rolling everything back. Compensation is still required where a completed step's visible effect makes the outcome worse than the baseline.

  • Constraint this puts on subsystems: **every step's effect must itself leave

the system in a valid state**. If a real step cannot guarantee that, it needs a compensation and a stricter flow.

  • Under a future partition both sides may proceed; convergence relies on

idempotent effects plus the deterministic better_than ordering (keep the maximum).

Consequences

  • Transaction becomes a flow interpreter plus an append-only event log.
  • Subsystem contract: command carries tx_id, step_id, attempt; reply is

ok(candidate), rejected(reason) (business) or error(reason) (retry).

  • Idempotency must be durable in subsystems, not an in-memory map.
  • The term-to-file Store is a spike artefact. A real transactional store

(outbox/inbox pattern) is required before production use.

  • Ordering semantics ("better than") become part of the flow schema and need

property-based tests.

Amendment 1 (2026-10-15): the real domain is provider selection, not a sequence

Decision 2 was clarified. The "subsystems" are facades over unreliable third-party providers (initial use case: FX swap + idempotent payment). Providers may be fast and cheap, slow, expensive, degraded, stalled, or change their behaviour without notice. We have no control over them.

This changes the shape of a flow from "A then B" to:

received → quoting → selecting → committing → settled
                │                    │
   (fan-out to N providers,          └─ timeout/unknown → in_doubt → reconciling → settled | failed
    deadline, collect quotes)

Principles

  1. Quote freely, commit once. Fan-out/hedging is safe for quotes (no

effect). Execution has exactly one commit point per transaction at a time. "Keep going" (decision 2) applies to quoting/selection and to choosing the next provider after a definite failure.

  1. A timeout on commit is not a failure, it is in_doubt. Before trying

another provider we must reconcile the first (query by idempotency key) or rely on a provider-honoured idempotency key. Never double-pay.

  1. Best-outcome-so-far lives in the pre-commit phase. The baseline is a

valid default route (e.g. last known-good provider); quotes can only improve it, judged by a scoring policy. After commit we only reconcile.

  1. Providers are modelled, not trusted. Each has our view of its state

(healthy | degraded | stalled | unknown | quarantined), observed version / payload fingerprint, rolling latency and error rate, and cost basis.

  1. A sentinel keeps the model honest: scheduled probes (configurable

interval, e.g. 6h) plus passive observation of live traffic, detecting version/shape drift, latency drift and availability, feeding a circuit breaker.

  1. Scoring is policy as data: weights/constraints over cost, latency,

reliability and quote validity window; hard constraints (e.g. quote must not expire before commit).

  1. Resource budgets are first-class: bounded in-flight work per provider

(bulkhead), bounded mailboxes, log compaction/retention, telemetry on CPU, memory, storage and network. Hedging has a network/rate-limit cost and is budgeted.

Provider adapter contract (behaviour)

  • quote(request) -> {:ok, quote} | {:rejected, why} | {:error, transient} | :timeout
  • execute(quote, idempotency_key) -> {:ok, receipt} | {:rejected, why} | {:error, transient} | :in_doubt
  • status(idempotency_key) -> {:ok, receipt} | :not_found | :unknown
  • probe() -> version / up-state / latency sample

Open questions

  • Do providers support lookup by idempotency key (resolves in_doubt)?
  • Are quotes firm with an expiry, or indicative?
  • Is the scoring objective global or per customer/request?
  • Are the FX and payment legs one provider call or two (second commit point)?

Amendment 2 (2026-10-15): lifecycle, cascading policy, settlement deadlines

Answers to the open questions:

  • Providers can look up a payment by idempotency key, but not in real time

yet. in_doubt resolution therefore needs an asynchronous reconcile path (poll/schedule) for now; revisit later.

  • Quotes are firm with an expiry: expiry is a hard constraint on every

later step.

  • The FX leg and the payment leg are separate provider calls, so a

transaction has several effectful steps, not one commit point.

Lifecycle (supersedes the single-commit sketch)

  → authorise payment (hold) → make payment
  → settlement phases, each with a regional cut-off (e.g. 18:00 UK local for UK payouts)
  • quote is hedgeable. accept, authorise, pay and settle are

effectful: each has its own idempotency key, own in-doubt handling and a defined compensation (cancel/unwind, possibly at a cost). Compensation is therefore real again after the first effectful step.

  • Each step carries a deadline: quote expiry for accept; regional local-time

Cascading objectives (policy as data)

Resolution order, most specific wins, others inherited: global default → merchant policy (optional, paid) → special customer policy.

Amendment 3 (2026-10-15): commit-step rules, undo operations, observability

Decided:

  • Compensation is modelled as real provider operations on the adapter

  • Doubt handling (Payment): status lookup first. Unconfirmed

  • Policy gains max_bps_from_best (hard constraint applied before scoring).
  • Observability is a first-class requirement. All components emit

ADR 0002: Commit-step safety rules

Status: accepted · Date: 2026-10-15 · Supersedes the "quote freely, commit once" sketch in ADR 0001 Amendment 1

Context

The real lifecycle has several effectful provider steps (accept, authorise, pay) and providers can answer slowly, wrongly or not at all. Naive failover on timeout risks paying twice; naive "fail" on timeout risks leaving money held.

Decision

  1. Quoting is free and hedged. Every later step is effectful and carries its own

idempotency key "<tx>:<provider>:<step>", reused on retry.

  1. Definite failures (technical error): bounded retry with the same key. Then

compensate what was done at that provider and fail over to the next-ranked quote.

  1. Doubt (timeout, answer lost): ask the provider (status(op, key)) before doing anything else.
  2. Accept / authorise (reversible): if still unconfirmed, cancel by key (idempotent, defeats a late effect) and fail over.
  3. Pay: never fail over while in doubt. Reconcile a bounded number of times, then needs_review.
  4. Compensation is a first-class provider operation (cancel_accept, void_authorisation, refund), keyed by the effect's key, idempotent, and safe for a key that never took effect.
  5. Compensation that cannot complete is escalated (needs_review), never ignored.

Alternatives considered

  • Fail over on any timeout: simplest, unsafe for money.
  • Retry pay on a different provider with a new key: double-payment risk.
  • Two-phase commit across providers: providers do not support it.

Consequences

  • A small, explicit set of states a human may have to resolve.
  • Providers must support idempotent keys and undo-by-key (assumed; to be validated against real APIs).
  • status is allowed to be stale, so "not found" means "not known yet".
  • The randomised storm test asserts these rules as invariants.

ADR 0003: Providers are modelled, not trusted

Status: accepted · Date: 2026-10-15

Context

Providers are third parties that decay, stall, are delayed or change behaviour without notice. The system must keep serving ("keep going") and protect money.

Decision

  • Each provider sits behind a Provider.Server with a circuit breaker, a rolling

health window, a bulkhead (max in-flight) and enforced per-call timeouts.

  • Health is a pure model (Provider.Health): unknown / healthy / degraded / stalled / quarantined.
  • Version drift quarantines a provider until released; behaviour change on a money path is not trusted.
  • Recovery is gradual: after a successful half-open probe, history is cleared and

the provider stays degraded until recovery_successes consecutive successes.

  • Selection never calls providers whose state the policy excludes.
  • A sentinel probes on an interval; in the spike it is a demo timer.

Alternatives considered

  • Trust provider responses: unsafe.
  • Reset history on recovery then declare healthy: a single lucky probe re-admits full traffic (rejected by the owner in favour of gradual).
  • Let old errors age out only: a quiet provider never recovers.

Consequences

  • The model is deterministic and exhaustively testable (time is injected).
  • Quarantine needs an operator or sentinel policy to release; review tooling is future work.

ADR 0007: Cascading policy, standing guarantees, deadlines and settlement

Status: accepted · Date: 2026-10-15

Context

The owner's objectives cascade (global at start-up, per merchant as a paid privilege, per special customer). Merchants may hold a weekly guaranteed rate, while we may route better or worse when transactions actually occur. Payments must be made as soon as possible and no later than a regional local-time cut-off (e.g. 18:00 UK), followed by settlement phases.

Decision

  1. PolicySet resolves global → merchant → customer. Overrides are partial (unspecified

fields inherited; weights merge key by key then renormalise). A merchant layer applies only when overrides_allowed is true; otherwise it is ignored and the resolution says so. Every layer is validated when the set is built. The layers used are narrated on every transaction.

  1. Standing guarantees are evaluated at completion: margin = achieved_net − amount × guaranteed_rate.

Positive margin and negative exposure are recorded idempotently per transaction in a Ledger, and exposure is narrated loudly. Routing is not constrained by the guarantee (the scoring already maximises net value); a guarantee-aware constraint is future work.

  1. Cut-offs (Cutoff) are local hour:minute plus a UTC offset that is a fixed integer **or a

function of the date, so DST rules can be supplied from a timezone database without this project taking a runtime dependency on one**. Absolute instants (Cutoff.fixed/2) exist for tests and the demo.

  1. Deadline enforcement: checked before every effectful step and failover.
  2. before payment: stop, undo what was done, failed {:deadline_missed, region};
  3. after payment, before settlement: needs_review {:settlement_overdue, region};
  4. a payment already in doubt keeps reconciling past the cut-off (money may have moved).
  5. Settlement is a final effectful step with no undo. Refusal or exhausted retries escalate to

needs_review {:settlement_failed, ...}; the payment is never refunded automatically. Answer lost → reconcile like pay; unresolved → needs_review {:in_doubt, :settle}.

Alternatives considered

  • Depend on tzdata: correct but adds the first runtime dependency; deferred behind the offset function.
  • Make guarantees a routing hard constraint: would reduce availability for a price we may choose to absorb.
  • Auto-refund on settlement failure: a refund is itself an effect with cost and risk; a human decides.

Consequences

  • Regional rules are reference data the caller must supply correctly (risk R14).
  • Margin accounting is in memory until the durable store exists.
  • More terminal states for a human to resolve (UC-4 becomes more important).

ADR 0008: Exact money, a tamper-evident audit chain, and an economics ledger

Status: accepted (2026-10-17)

Context

Three needs arrived together. A payments system must not hold money in floats. Auditors (PCI DSS requirement 10, financial recordkeeping) expect a complete, ordered, tamper-evident record. And the business wants to know what the system costs to run and what better rates save or earn.

Decisions

1. Money is integers (ActorTxSpike.Money)

Amounts and fees are integer minor units; rates are integers scaled by 10^8. Decimal input (literals, strings) is converted exactly at the edge through its shortest representation, never through binary floating point. Rounding is half-even. Display helpers exist for narration and the dashboard; nothing computes with them. Floats remain only for non-money ratios (scores, basis-point comparisons, error rates).

2. The audit log is an append-only hash chain (ActorTxSpike.Audit)

Every event becomes one JSON line committing to the previous record's hash. Editing, removing, inserting or reordering is detected at the first affected line (mix audit.verify). The log refuses to start on a log that fails verification. Free-form fields are scrubbed of card-like numbers (Luhn-valid, 13-19 digits) before they are written. Known limit: truncating the tail needs an externally anchored head to detect.

3. Cost and value are separate from the orchestrator (Costs, Economics)

Payment only measures: reductions, memory, duration, bytes in and out (a proxy: the external term size of each call), state changes, provider calls, compensations, plus price discovery (chosen net against the median, worst and best quote) and guarantee margin. It emits one tx_economics event. Economics prices that with an explicit rate card and keeps totals, overall and per provider. Costs are in pico-units (10^-12 of a major unit) because one transaction costs a tiny fraction of a cent. Prices are placeholders to be replaced by invoice figures.

"Saved" always has a stated baseline (the median quote). The price of non-price goals (regret vs best) is shown, not hidden.

Consequences

  • Rounding is now explicit and testable; guarantees, ledger and selection are exact.
  • Raw measurements live in the audit log, so any cost figure can be recomputed under a

different rate card.

  • Cost attribution covers the orchestrator process only; provider-side and VM-wide cost is

covered by the platform line and the Metrics sampler, not per transaction.

  • Costs and values are assumed to share a reporting currency.
  • The audit writer is not yet supervised or fail-closed (see compliance gaps).

ADR 0010: Write-ahead log, fail closed, and crash recovery

Status: accepted (2026-10-17)

Context

Everything lived in memory: a restart lost every in-flight transaction, and money could be half-moved with no record of it. The audit log was written after the fact, by a writer that could die unnoticed. Forwards and regulated rollout both need transactions to survive a restart, and the evidence to be written before the effect, not after.

Decisions

1. The log is written ahead of every money-moving call

Events.register_sink/3 lets a durable sink (the audit log, or the regional router) register. Events.barrier/1 returns only once every sink has written everything emitted before the call. Payment calls it before each accept, authorise, pay, settle and each undo. So after a crash the log is never missing a call that was actually sent:

  • a call in the log may have reached the provider (the answer may be lost): it is

reconciled by status, never repeated blindly;

  • a call not in the log was never sent: it is safe to make.

Status checks and quote requests are not write-ahead (they move no money). The barrier is called by the transaction, not the event server, so a slow sink cannot wedge the stream.

2. Fail closed

If a registered sink has died, barrier fails until a replacement registers under the same name; if it is merely unresponsive, that call fails. Payment then moves no money: a transaction that has done nothing yet fails (audit_unavailable); one that has moved money goes to needs_review. A sink that stops on purpose unregisters first; a crash does not.

3. One writer per log, group commit

  • A chain has one head, so each log takes <log>.lock naming its OS and Erlang process; a

second writer is refused, and a lock whose owner is gone is taken over. (A real overlap of two instances on one log was seen during testing and broke the chain.)

  • Records are written at once and made durable together: one fsync on each barrier and at the

latest every 50 ms, instead of one per record, which capped throughput at the disk's fsync rate and starved the barrier under load.

4. Recovery from the log (ActorTxSpike.Recovery)

Recovery.plan/1 is a pure function from one transaction's records to where it stood.

The money rules then apply unchanged: an unresolved pay or settle is never failed over; an unconfirmed reversible step is cancelled by key. A transaction whose provider no longer exists, or whose quote was never recorded, is escalated, not guessed.

Evidence it works

Pure plan tests for every position; resume against a real run cut off at nine points (nothing sent, and in-doubt and done for each of accept, authorise, pay, settle) plus before choosing, mid-undo, after the last undo, and after a missed cut-off; random kills of a running transaction at ten moments asserting at most one payment whatever the timing; a restart from regional logs; and a demo that resumes a transaction killed mid-flight.

5. The audit log is part of the supervision tree, and mandatory in production

config :actor_tx_spike, audit: [dir:, logs:] starts Audit.Router right after the event stream, supervised and permanent. If it dies the barrier fails (fail closed), the supervisor restarts it, and it re-registers; the lock of the dead writer is taken over. A restarted router rebuilds its routing table from the logs, so the rest of an open local-only transaction does not leak into the global log. require_audit: true (set in production) makes the application refuse to start without an :audit setting.

Limits (stated plainly)

  • Fail closed holds only while a sink is registered. Development and most tests run with

no audit log, unlogged by design. Production is protected by require_audit; nothing stops someone configuring audit with an empty or wrong logs list (a decision naming a log the router lacks crashes the router, which is the safe failure).

  • A pay that was never sent but is in the log as started is reconciled as in doubt, and an

unprovable "not found" for pay goes to a human. Conservative by design, so a crash in that window costs a human review rather than risking a double payment.

  • A resumed transaction's measured cost covers only its second life; its policy label is not

restored; a resumed quote may have expired, which then fails over normally.

  • The simulated demo world forgets its provider state on restart (it is a simulation of the

outside world); real providers would remember.

  • Recovery needs the provider registry at restart to contain the same provider ids.
  • Nothing is rotated, archived or deleted yet; retention is recorded, not enforced.

ADR 0011: Jurisdiction as a hard constraint, and regional logs

Status: accepted (2026-10-17)

Context

The scope is spot and forward, business and some retail customers, across eleven jurisdictions (UK, EU, US, India, Mexico, Brazil, Turkey, Saudi Arabia, Japan, South Korea, China). Regulation decides which providers may carry a corridor, which instruments and customer types are allowed, and where the records may live. Those are not preferences to weigh in a score. See docs/compliance/COMPLIANCE_EVIDENCE.md.

Decisions

1. Eligibility is applied before scoring (ActorTxSpike.Eligibility)

Declarative, validated rules per region plus a default. A transaction touches the regions named by its origin_region and destination_region; the strictest result wins: an instrument or customer type must be allowed by all, a provider by all, retention is the longest, and records go to the union of the regions' logs. Ineligible providers are never asked (they should not receive the request). A transaction whose instrument or customer type is not permitted stops before any provider is called. The decision, including who was ruled out and why, is recorded in tx_received, so replay can prove it (eligibility_respected).

2. Regional logs (Audit.Router)

One hash-chained log per named region (<dir>/<name>.log), each independently verifiable and deliverable. A region rule may be local_only: a transaction touching it is recorded only in the local-only regions' logs, not in the global one. Events without a transaction (health, operator actions) go to GLOBAL. The router is one durable sink, so the write-ahead barrier covers every region. A decision naming a log the router does not have crashes the router (fail closed): the record has nowhere lawful to go. A regional log that dies takes the router with it.

3. Residency is provable (Replay.Residency, mix replay residency DIR)

Given the logs, every transaction must appear in exactly the logs its own decision named: not in one it must not be in (a leak), not missing from one it must be in.

Limits (stated plainly)

  • The rules are caller-supplied data and are real only once counsel confirms them per country;

the demo's IN and CN rules are illustrative.

  • Retention days are recorded in the decision but not enforced: no rotation, archive or

deletion exists. Deleting a prefix of a chain needs an anchored head to keep it verifiable.

  • A corridor that touches a local-only region leaves the other region without a record of that

transaction; whether that satisfies the other region's rules is a legal question.

  • Sanctions and KYC gates, forward deal lifecycle, trade reporting and the retail flows are not

built; instrument is only an eligibility attribute today.

  • Cut-off rules, policy cascade and guarantees are still per transaction, not per jurisdiction.

How code is shaped to fit

  • Decide in function heads, not in bodies. Several clauses with patterns and guards instead

of case/cond inside a function (Payment.run_step/3, Recovery.stood_at/2, Selector.outcome/2, Fake.effect_result/3).

  • Table-driven instead of branchy. A list of {name, key, parse} fields walked by one

  • Name the predicate. A condition with and/or becomes a function whose name says what

it means (Replay.Rules.unresolved_money_doubt?/1, Policy.too_slow?/2).

  • Optional things get a clause. nil-tolerant reads are defp field(nil, _), do: nil,

not x && x[...] (each &&, ||, and, or counts as a branch).

  • Pipeline of small named steps for a procedure (Selector.select/4: partition, observe,

evaluate, build, narrate).

  • Waiting is one function (Poll.until/2), never a hand-rolled cond with Process.sleep.

  • Reading a function never needs more than four decisions held in the head.
  • More, smaller functions and a few more modules (Validation, Parser, Narrator, Poll).

  • New code that exceeds a limit fails CI. Whole-file escapes (credo:disable-for-this-file) are

not used and should not be added.

  • The limits measure shape, not correctness. They do not replace review or the invariant tests.

Alternatives considered

  • Keep Credo's defaults (complexity 9): passes today but permits the functions that were hardest to read.
  • Complexity 3: 70 functions in lib/ and test/ would need splitting, mostly dispatch tables that are clearer as one function with several clauses; the gain stops paying for the fragmentation. 4 is the lowest value where a plain case with a few clauses still fits.
  • A dedicated complexity tool outside Credo: more tooling for the same signal.

ADR 0013: Back-pressure: admission control, priority bulkheads and waiting instead of failing

Status: accepted · Date: 2026-10-22

Context

The first burst test (1,000 randomised transactions, no pause) showed the system degrading by failing customers: most ended with "no provider could give a usable quote", a few with overloaded accepts, and 29 in needs_review because undo and settle calls were refused. Nothing limited how many transactions ran at once, each transaction fans out to every provider, and each provider's bulkhead holds 10 calls, so a burst saturated every provider at once. Undo and settle calls, which protect money already moved, competed for slots with brand-new quote requests.

Decision

Back-pressure in four layers, each safe because an overloaded answer means the call was never made:

  1. Admission control (ActorTxSpike.Admission). At most max_in_flight transactions run at once. A submission over

the limit is refused immediately with {:error, {:overloaded, retry_after_ms}}, before any provider is asked or anything is recorded. A slot is held from admission until the payment ends or its process dies. A running id submitted again is the same transaction and does not count twice. Default: no limit; production sets 100 (ACTOR_TX_MAX_IN_FLIGHT). Changeable at run time.

  1. Priority classes in the provider bulkhead (Provider.Server). Quote requests (new, sheddable work) may use 60% of the

slots; accept, authorise, pay and settle (a transaction already under way) 100%; undo, status and probe 150%. Work already in motion and the calls that protect money are not starved by new work.

  1. Waiting instead of giving up. A busy provider is asked for a quote again every 15 ms until the selection deadline.

A busy provider on a step (accept, authorise, pay, settle) is asked again after the backoff without using up an attempt; the transaction's deadline bounds the wait (settlement past its cut-off still goes to review).

  1. A sender that backs off (Demo.Bulk). A refused submission is held and retried after the suggested pause (counted as

throttled); an optional sender cap makes the sender wait while that many of its own transactions are running.

Consequences

  • Under load the system completes what it admits, and tells callers to wait for the rest. Measured: with a limit of 100,

1,000 of 1,000 and 1,995 of 2,000 complete; with no limit 30% of a 2,000 burst completes (LOAD_RESULTS).

  • The refusal is cheap and clean: no record, no provider call, nothing to undo. Callers must handle {:overloaded, ms}.
  • Throughput is bounded by the limit; choosing it is an operational decision that needs measuring against real providers.
  • More waiting means a transaction can take up to its deadline under load before it ends; quotes and steps hold their

place instead of failing.

  • Recovery-resumed transactions bypass admission (they were already admitted before the restart).
  • Not done: a queue in front of the limit (refusal pushes the wait to the caller), automatic limit tuning, per-merchant

fairness, and a replay record of refusals (they leave no trace in the audit log by design).

Alternatives considered

  • Queue submissions inside the system. Hides overload from callers, grows memory, and breaks the soft-real-time bound.
  • Do nothing but bigger bulkheads. Moves the cliff, does not remove it; real providers set their own limits.
  • Fail fast on quotes (the previous behaviour). Safe but fails customers who would have been served a few ms later.

ADR 0014: Product depth: currencies drive pricing and eligibility, a review workflow, recorded batches

Status: accepted · Date: 2026-10-23

Context

Three gaps stood between the engine and something a business could operate:

  1. Currencies were decoration. A request could name a source and destination currency but nothing used it: every provider

quoted one rate, every provider was asked, and no region could say which currencies it permits.

  1. needs_review was a dead end. The system rightly stopped when it could not be sure what happened to money, but nobody

could do anything about it: no queue, no way to ask the provider, no way to repeat a safe step, and no record of a person's decision (use case UC-4 was unbuilt).

  1. Bulk batches left no trace in the audit log beyond the transactions they made, so a replayed day could not show what had

been sent on purpose, and why.

Decision

Currencies

  • A request's source_currency and destination_currency price, route and permit it.
  • Pricing. A provider quotes a pair at the market (mid) rate carried through its own spread (Provider.Fake: mid * rate / ref_rate),

so repricing, drift and healing move every pair. Requests without currencies, and unknown pairs, are quoted as before.

  • Capability. A provider lists the currencies it carries. The selector does not ask a provider that lacks either currency

(and says so in the story and in excluded); with no capable provider there is no quote.

  • Eligibility. A region's rule may list currencies; both of a transaction's currencies must be allowed in every region of its

corridor (the strictest wins), and the reasons name the currency and the region.

  • Not done: currencies with different minor-unit exponents (JPY, KRW have none; the demo treats all as two decimals), cross-currency

fee schedules, and holding or converting balances.

Review workflow

  • A transaction that ends in needs_review waits in a queue (ActorTxSpike.Review), open until a person closes it.
  • A person can check with the provider (read-only status of each step's key), retry a step that is safe to repeat under its

original key (settle and the undos), and resolve with an outcome (paid, not_paid, written_off), saying who and why.

  • Write-ahead. Every action is emitted and flushed to the audit log before it takes effect; if the log cannot take it, nothing is

sent and nothing changes. A decision without a name or a reason is refused.

  • Same logs. A transaction's review actions are recorded in the regional logs the transaction was routed to: the audit router keeps

routing a transaction that went to review until its resolution is recorded, and rebuilds that after a restart.

  • Rebuildable. The queue is pure data (Review.Queue) built from live events or from audit-log records, so Review.load/1 restores

it after a restart and mix replay reviews reads it from a log alone.

  • Checkable in hindsight. A replay rule (review_recorded) fails a resolution that lacks an outcome, a name or a reason, or that is

recorded for a transaction that never went to review.

  • Not done: four-eyes approval, assignment and queues per team, deadlines on open reviews, economics adjustments for a manual resolution,

and retrying steps that need a quote (accept, authorise, pay).

Recorded batches

  • Starting and ending a Bulk send batch are recorded as bulk_started (the whole plan, in minor units, with seed and settings) and

bulk_finished (how it ended and the counts). Replay.batches/1 and mix replay batches read them back; state_at includes the batches so far.

Deferred: re-execution replay

Re-running the orchestrator against recorded provider answers on a virtual clock (to rebuild a running world at a moment, and to test a changed rule on behaviour rather than on quotes alone) is a project of its own and is not part of this change.

Consequences

  • Corridors behave like corridors: a dollar-to-rupee payment is priced near 83, only providers that carry rupees are asked, and India

and China accept only the currencies their rules list.

  • Random batches now include currency-refused combinations as well as instrument and customer-type ones.
  • People can finish what the system refused to guess, and the evidence of who decided what and why is in the same tamper-evident log.
  • Review depends on the audit log being up for every action (fail closed), consistent with the payment path.
  • Provider capability and a region's currency list are separate ideas that can disagree (a region may allow a currency no eligible

provider carries); that case ends in "no provider could give a usable quote", by design.

Alternatives considered

  • Let a reviewer edit the transaction's state directly. Simple, but it would bypass the write-ahead log and the provider's idempotency.
  • A reviewer UI that only reads. Does not resolve anything; the point is a recorded decision.
  • Reject unsupported pairs at the provider (ask and get "no"). Wastes a call and a bulkhead slot per provider per transaction.
